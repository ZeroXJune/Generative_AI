LESSON: CALCULUS I - LIMITS, DERIVATIVES, AND INTEGRALS

COURSE LEVEL: Undergraduate, first year
PREREQUISITES: Algebra II, Trigonometry

THE LIMIT
=========
A limit describes the value a function approaches as its input approaches some
point. Formally, the limit of f(x) as x approaches a equals L if, for every
epsilon greater than zero, there exists a delta greater than zero such that
whenever the distance from x to a is less than delta, the distance from f(x) to
L is less than epsilon.

A function is continuous at a point when three conditions hold: f(a) is
defined, the limit exists, and the limit equals f(a).

THE DERIVATIVE
==============
The derivative measures instantaneous rate of change. It is defined as the
limit of the difference quotient:

  f'(x) = limit as h approaches 0 of [f(x + h) - f(x)] / h

Geometrically, the derivative at a point is the slope of the tangent line.

Core differentiation rules:
- Power rule: the derivative of x^n is n times x^(n-1)
- Product rule: (fg)' = f'g + fg'
- Quotient rule: (f/g)' = (f'g - fg') / g^2
- Chain rule: the derivative of f(g(x)) is f'(g(x)) times g'(x)

Key derivatives: the derivative of sin x is cos x; of cos x is negative sin x;
of e^x is e^x; of the natural log of x is 1/x.

APPLICATIONS OF THE DERIVATIVE
==============================
- Critical points occur where the derivative equals zero or is undefined
- The first derivative test identifies local maxima and minima
- The second derivative determines concavity; a positive second derivative
  means concave up
- Optimization problems minimize or maximize a quantity subject to constraints
- Related rates connect the rates of change of two linked quantities

THE INTEGRAL
============
Integration is the inverse operation of differentiation. The definite integral
represents accumulated quantity, defined as the limit of Riemann sums as the
partition width approaches zero. Geometrically it is the signed area between
the curve and the horizontal axis.

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus links the two operations. Part one states
that if F is defined as the integral of f from a to x, then F'(x) = f(x). Part
two states that the integral of f from a to b equals F(b) - F(a), where F is
any antiderivative of f.

Techniques of integration include substitution (reversing the chain rule),
integration by parts (reversing the product rule), partial fractions for
rational functions, and trigonometric substitution.

WHY IT MATTERS
==============
Calculus is the mathematical language of change. Physics uses derivatives for
velocity and acceleration, economics for marginal cost and revenue, biology
for population growth, and machine learning for gradient descent, where the
derivative of a loss function indicates the direction of steepest improvement.

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
=====================
- A function can be continuous without being differentiable; the absolute
  value function at zero is the standard example
- A zero derivative does not guarantee a maximum or minimum; it may be an
  inflection point, as with x^3 at the origin
- The definite integral yields signed area, so regions below the axis
  contribute negatively
