LESSON: ACADEMIC WRITING AND COMPOSITION

COURSE LEVEL: Undergraduate, first year
PREREQUISITES: None

PURPOSE AND AUDIENCE
====================
Academic writing makes a claim and supports it with evidence for a reader who
is intelligent but not already convinced. Every structural choice follows from
that purpose.

THE THESIS STATEMENT
====================
A thesis is a single arguable claim that the essay will defend. It belongs
near the end of the introduction.

A thesis must be arguable rather than factual. "The Second World War ended in
1945" is a fact and cannot be a thesis. "Economic provisions of the Versailles
Treaty contributed more to German instability than its territorial clauses" is
arguable, specific, and defensible.

Weak theses are vague, announce rather than argue ("This essay will discuss"),
or state the obvious.

PARAGRAPH STRUCTURE
===================
A standard body paragraph contains a topic sentence stating the paragraph's
claim, evidence supporting it, analysis explaining how the evidence supports
the claim, and a transition.

The analysis is the part most often omitted and the part that matters most.
Quoted evidence does not interpret itself; the writer must say what it shows
and why it matters to the thesis.

ESSAY ORGANISATION
==================
Introduction: establish context, narrow to the problem, state the thesis.

Body: one claim per paragraph, ordered so each builds on the last. Address the
strongest counterargument rather than the weakest.

Conclusion: synthesise rather than merely summarise. State what follows from
the argument, and what remains unresolved.

EVIDENCE AND CITATION
=====================
Primary sources are original materials: data, documents, interviews, works
under study. Secondary sources interpret primary ones. Scholarly sources are
peer-reviewed.

Citation serves three purposes: it credits the originator, it lets readers
verify claims, and it situates the work within existing scholarship.

Common styles: MLA in the humanities, using author and page; APA in the social
sciences, using author and date; Chicago, which supports both notes and
author-date formats. Consistency within a document matters more than the choice
between them.

Plagiarism is the presentation of another's work or ideas as one's own. It
includes unattributed paraphrase, not just copied wording. Paraphrasing still
requires citation.

REVISION
========
Revision is structural: does the argument hold, is the evidence sufficient, is
the order logical. Editing is sentence-level. Proofreading catches errors.
These are distinct activities, and doing them simultaneously does all three
badly.

Practical techniques: outline the draft you actually wrote and compare it to
the one you intended; read aloud to expose awkward syntax; cut any sentence
that does not advance the argument.

CLARITY
=======
- Prefer active voice unless the actor is genuinely unimportant
- Prefer concrete nouns and strong verbs to abstraction
- Place the main clause first
- Delete intensifiers that add no information: very, really, quite
- Define technical terms on first use

WHY IT MATTERS
==============
Writing is how academic and professional work is evaluated and transmitted. The
discipline of making a claim and defending it with evidence is itself a form of
thinking, not merely its presentation.

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
=====================
- Longer sentences and larger vocabulary do not signal sophistication; clarity
  does
- The five-paragraph form is a teaching scaffold, not a requirement
- A conclusion that only restates the introduction wastes the reader's
  attention
