LESSON: LOGIC AND CRITICAL THINKING

COURSE LEVEL: Undergraduate, first year
PREREQUISITES: None

ARGUMENTS
=========
An argument consists of premises offered in support of a conclusion. It is not
a quarrel, and not a mere assertion.

Deductive arguments claim that the conclusion follows necessarily from the
premises. Inductive arguments claim only that the conclusion is probable given
the premises.

VALIDITY AND SOUNDNESS
======================
A deductive argument is valid when the conclusion follows necessarily from the
premises. Validity concerns structure alone, not truth.

A valid argument can have false premises and a false conclusion. For example:
all birds are mammals; all mammals have scales; therefore all birds have
scales. The structure is valid; every statement is false.

An argument is sound when it is both valid and has all true premises. A sound
argument necessarily has a true conclusion.

For inductive arguments the corresponding terms are strength and cogency.

COMMON VALID FORMS
==================
- Modus ponens: if P then Q; P; therefore Q
- Modus tollens: if P then Q; not Q; therefore not P
- Hypothetical syllogism: if P then Q; if Q then R; therefore if P then R
- Disjunctive syllogism: either P or Q; not P; therefore Q

COMMON INVALID FORMS
====================
- Affirming the consequent: if P then Q; Q; therefore P. Invalid, because Q may
  have another cause.
- Denying the antecedent: if P then Q; not P; therefore not Q. Also invalid.

These two closely resemble the valid forms above, which is precisely why they
persuade.

INFORMAL FALLACIES
==================
- Ad hominem: attacking the arguer rather than the argument
- Straw man: misrepresenting a position to make it easier to refute
- False dilemma: presenting two options when more exist
- Appeal to authority: citing authority outside its domain of expertise
- Post hoc: inferring causation from mere sequence
- Circular reasoning: assuming the conclusion among the premises
- Slippery slope: asserting an extreme outcome without supporting the chain
- Hasty generalisation: drawing a broad conclusion from insufficient cases
- Appeal to ignorance: treating absence of disproof as proof

EVALUATING EVIDENCE
===================
Useful questions: What is the claim, precisely? What evidence supports it? What
is the source, and what interests does it have? Could the evidence be explained
otherwise? What evidence would count against the claim?

A claim that no possible observation could refute is unfalsifiable, and
unfalsifiability is a weakness rather than a strength.

Burden of proof rests with whoever makes a positive claim. Extraordinary claims
require proportionately strong evidence.

CORRELATION AND CAUSATION
=========================
An observed association may arise because one variable causes the other,
because the causation runs the other way, because a third variable causes both
(confounding), or by coincidence.

Establishing causation generally requires controlled experiment, a plausible
mechanism, correct temporal ordering, and the elimination of confounds.

WHY IT MATTERS
==============
Logic is the shared standard of reasoning across disciplines. It is also the
practical basis for evaluating news, advertising, and political claims, and it
underlies formal reasoning in mathematics and computer science.

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
=====================
- Validity does not mean truth; a valid argument can be entirely false
- Identifying a fallacy in an argument does not establish that its conclusion
  is false, only that this argument fails to support it
- Inductive arguments are not defective for being uncertain; probability is
  their proper standard
