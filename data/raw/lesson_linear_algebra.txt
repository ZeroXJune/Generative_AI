LESSON: LINEAR ALGEBRA - VECTORS, MATRICES, AND EIGENVALUES

COURSE LEVEL: Undergraduate, first or second year
PREREQUISITES: Algebra II

VECTORS AND VECTOR SPACES
=========================
A vector is an ordered list of numbers representing magnitude and direction, or
equivalently a point in n-dimensional space. A vector space is a set closed
under vector addition and scalar multiplication, satisfying eight axioms
including associativity, commutativity of addition, and the existence of a zero
vector and additive inverses.

Key operations:
- Dot product: the sum of componentwise products. Produces a scalar.
- Norm (length): the square root of the dot product of a vector with itself
- Cosine of the angle between vectors: the dot product divided by the product
  of the two norms
- Two vectors are orthogonal when their dot product is zero

MATRICES
========
A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers representing a linear
transformation. Matrix multiplication composes transformations and is
associative but NOT commutative: AB generally does not equal BA.

Important properties:
- The identity matrix has ones on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere
- A matrix is invertible if and only if its determinant is nonzero
- The transpose exchanges rows and columns
- Rank is the number of linearly independent rows, equivalently columns

SOLVING LINEAR SYSTEMS
======================
A system of linear equations is written Ax = b. Gaussian elimination reduces
the augmented matrix to row echelon form through three elementary row
operations: swapping rows, scaling a row, and adding a multiple of one row to
another.

A system has exactly one solution when the rank of A equals the number of
unknowns, infinitely many when the rank is smaller and the system is
consistent, and none when the system is inconsistent.

LINEAR INDEPENDENCE AND BASIS
=============================
Vectors are linearly independent when no one of them can be written as a
combination of the others. A basis is a linearly independent set that spans the
space. The dimension of a space is the number of vectors in any basis.

EIGENVALUES AND EIGENVECTORS
============================
An eigenvector of A is a nonzero vector v whose direction is unchanged by the
transformation: Av = lambda times v, where lambda is the eigenvalue.

Eigenvalues are found by solving the characteristic equation: the determinant
of (A minus lambda times the identity) equals zero.

Eigendecomposition expresses a matrix in terms of its eigenvalues and
eigenvectors, which reveals the transformation's fundamental scaling behaviour
along its invariant directions.

WHY IT MATTERS
==============
Linear algebra underpins computer graphics (transformations as matrices),
machine learning (data as vectors, weights as matrices), Principal Component
Analysis (eigenvectors of the covariance matrix), and search engines
(PageRank is an eigenvector problem).

In text retrieval, documents become vectors and similarity becomes a dot
product or cosine. For unit-length vectors the dot product equals the cosine of
the angle between them, which is why normalisation matters so much in practice.

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
=====================
- Matrix multiplication is not commutative
- A determinant of zero means the transformation collapses space into a lower
  dimension and cannot be inverted
- Eigenvectors are not unique; any nonzero scalar multiple is also an
  eigenvector for the same eigenvalue
