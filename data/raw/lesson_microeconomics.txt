LESSON: MICROECONOMICS - SUPPLY, DEMAND, AND MARKET STRUCTURE

COURSE LEVEL: Undergraduate, first year
PREREQUISITES: Algebra, graph interpretation

CORE CONCEPTS
=============
Scarcity is the fundamental economic condition: wants exceed available
resources, so choices must be made.

Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative forgone. It is the
true cost of any decision, and it includes implicit as well as explicit costs.

Marginal analysis evaluates decisions by comparing the additional benefit of
one more unit against its additional cost. A rational actor continues an
activity while marginal benefit exceeds marginal cost.

Sunk costs are expenditures already incurred and unrecoverable. They should not
influence forward-looking decisions.

DEMAND
======
The law of demand states that, holding other factors constant, quantity
demanded falls as price rises. The demand curve therefore slopes downward.

Shifts in demand, as distinct from movement along the curve, arise from changes
in consumer income, the prices of substitutes and complements, tastes,
expectations, and the number of buyers.

SUPPLY
======
The law of supply states that quantity supplied rises as price rises, so the
supply curve slopes upward.

Shifts in supply arise from changes in input prices, technology, the number of
sellers, taxes and subsidies, and producer expectations.

MARKET EQUILIBRIUM
==================
Equilibrium occurs where the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied,
determining market price and quantity.

A price above equilibrium creates surplus, pushing price down. A price below
equilibrium creates shortage, pushing price up.

Price ceilings, set below equilibrium, create persistent shortages. Price
floors, set above equilibrium, create persistent surpluses. Minimum wage is a
price floor in the labour market.

ELASTICITY
==========
Price elasticity of demand measures responsiveness: the percentage change in
quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price.

Demand is elastic when the absolute value exceeds one, meaning quantity
responds strongly. It is inelastic when below one. Goods with many substitutes,
and non-necessities, tend to be elastic; necessities tend to be inelastic.

Elasticity determines who bears a tax. The more inelastic side of the market
bears more of the burden.

MARKET STRUCTURES
=================
Perfect competition: many small firms, identical products, free entry and exit,
and price-taking behaviour. Long-run economic profit tends toward zero.

Monopoly: a single seller with barriers to entry. The firm is a price maker and
produces less at a higher price than a competitive market would, generating
deadweight loss.

Monopolistic competition: many firms selling differentiated products, as in
restaurants or clothing.

Oligopoly: few firms, interdependent decisions, often analysed with game
theory. The prisoner's dilemma explains why cartels are unstable.

MARKET FAILURE
==============
Externalities are costs or benefits falling on third parties. Pollution is a
negative externality; vaccination carries a positive one. Unpriced
externalities lead markets to over- or under-produce.

Public goods are non-excludable and non-rival, which creates free-rider
problems and typically justifies public provision.

WHY IT MATTERS
==============
Microeconomics explains pricing, wages, regulation, and the tradeoffs behind
public policy.

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS
=====================
- A change in price moves along a demand curve; it does not shift it
- Economic profit differs from accounting profit because it subtracts implicit
  opportunity costs
- Monopolies do not charge infinite prices; they are still bound by demand
